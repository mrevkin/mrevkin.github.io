Insurgent Legality

Law in the Making and Breaking of the Islamic State in Iraq and Syria

Mara R. Revkin

Book manuscript in progress

Summary

When the Islamic State captured Mosul, Iraq’s second-largest city, in June 2014 over the course of only six days of fighting with little resistance from the Iraqi Army, humanitarian organizations expected a massive exodus. A huge number of people—as many as 500,000 out of a population of around 1.2 million—did flee Mosul by car and on foot in what was described at the time as “one of the largest and swiftest mass movements of people in the world in recent memory.” After just a few days, tens of thousands of those who had initially fled began to return to the city voluntarily after they heard from friends and family who had stayed behind that—contrary to their fears—the Islamic State “is not harming the people,” the city was “calm,” and life was returning to “normal.” Eight months after the group captured the city, an estimated 75 percent of Mosul’s population was still living there.

Why did so many people stay in Mosul and other areas captured by the Islamic State when it was still possible to leave? This question is important because all insurgent groups that seek to control territory rely heavily on the cooperation of civilians to provide information, labor, and services, and material support including taxes, food, and shelter. The Islamic State could not have captured and controlled as much territory as it did for more than three years without the cooperation, whether voluntary or involuntary, of a critical mass of the millions of Syrians and Iraqis who were living in the territory captured by the group in 2014. The prevailing explanations for its rise—its recruitment of foreign fighters, extreme violence, and capture of resources including oil and military stockpiles—do not fully explain the group’s ability to appeal to local Syrian and Iraqi civilians.

This book provides a supplementary explanation grounded in my concept of “insurgent legality.” Legality, as I define it, is a property of a governing authority’s conduct: the degree to which its behavior conforms to its own proclaimed rules. A legal order—whether democratic or authoritarian, secular or theocratic, recognized or insurgent—possesses legality insofar as its rules are public, prospective, intelligible, and consistently applied, and insofar as the conduct of its officials is congruent with them. The definition follows Lon Fuller’s account of the “internal morality of law,” particularly his requirement of congruence between official action and declared rule.

Drawing on insights from the political science literatures on state formation and rebel governance and legal scholarship on procedural justice, I develop a theory of insurgent legality that helps to explain why law is so important for the success or failure of revolutionary state-building projects that seek to overthrow existing social and political orders. Studies of the emergence of states in a variety of historical and regional contexts have converged on a key finding: states that are able to establish legality in the eyes of their citizens, including authoritarian legality, can maintain social and political control more easily than states that rely on violence and coercion alone. Importantly, legality is not the same thing as normative legitimacy. Authoritarian regimes can have a high degree of legality in terms of their predictability and procedural justice, but still not be perceived by their citizens as normatively legitimate, in the sense of having a moral right to rule. Legality is a relative rather than absolute concept, and citizens’ perceptions of the legality of any particular regime depend on comparisons with the previous status quo and available alternatives in the present.

I argue that legality is particularly important in civil wars and failing states where citizens tend to prioritize their most basic needs for survival in the short-term over political and social rights—such as freedom of speech and religion—that are normatively very important, but less urgent in the short-term. However, legality is difficult for any organization to maintain and requires a strong chain of command, codes of conduct, and institutions for training, discipline, and accountability mechanisms for members who break the rules. It is particularly difficult to maintain if an organization is growing rapidly or operating under pressure from a war or other emergency. If legality deteriorates over time, an insurgent group will tend to lose internal support from its own members, which can be observed in increased infighting between rival factions, desertions, and defections. The loss of legality will also lead to a loss of external support and increased resistance from the local population, either peaceful or violent, which tends to accelerate the collapse of an insurgent group. My theory is not monocausal, and I acknowledge that legality interacted with other factors to influence the rise and eventual collapse of the Islamic State including economic resources, technology, and military capabilities. My more limited claim is that legality is an important supplementary explanation that is often overlooked in the study of insurgencies.

The Islamic State’s theory of statecraft, articulated most clearly in its treatise entitled “Principles in the Administration of the Islamic State,” required a particular sequence of state-building activities in which legal institutions and rules were established first—immediately after the capture of new territory—along with essential services such as the distribution of subsidized bread. One of the first steps taken by the group upon capturing new territory was to announce its version of a social contract in the form of its “Charter of the City” (وثيقة المدينة), a constitution-like document that described a system of governance based on reciprocal obligations between the caliph and the people. Only after establishing a legal system based on the Charter did it eventually—months later—begin to implement extractive and unpopular policies including heavy taxes and forced military conscription that were necessary to sustain its military and state-building operations. By establishing legality first, the Islamic State laid the foundations for a kind of social contract that was authoritarian and asymmetric, but nonetheless was initially perceived by many Syrians and Iraqis as providing more security and predictability than the governments it had ousted.

To analyze the establishment of institutions and the imposition of policies and laws across time and space, I constructed an original dataset, the Islamic State Law and Governance Dataset, that documents the activities of the group’s many different institutions and policies in eight categories: courts; laws; police; taxes and other revenue-extracting policies; municipal services such as electricity and public sanitation; education, including preaching, schools, and adult religious education; healthcare; and military conscription. It is based on more than 2,000 primary source documents, almost all in Arabic, including Syrian newspaper articles, reports by local human rights organizations, social media posts by known Syrian journalists, activists, and other eyewitnesses in or near areas under the group’s control, and documents the Islamic State issued for day-to-day administrative and governance activities, such as traffic tickets, real estate deeds, and birth or marriage certificates.

The second half of the book concerns the Islamic State’s decline. I find evidence that its legality had already begun to erode many months before the U.S.-led coalition began military operations with airstrikes in Iraq on August 8, 2014 and in Syria on September 22, 2014. Over time, the group became increasingly repressive, violent, and unforgiving in its strict enforcement of the rules. By 2015, cigarette sellers who would have been let off with a warning in the early days of its rule were being thrown into prison and publicly beaten. A December 2014 fatwa restricted exit from its territory and a March 2015 decree declared anyone leaving Mosul an apostate, a capital offense that amounted to a travel ban. While the quality of its governance deteriorated dramatically, the group was becoming more violent toward civilians and also increased its extractive demands on them, imposing new taxes to cover the rising costs of its military operations and mandatory military service for all men and boys 14 and older. This combination—fewer benefits and lower-quality services for civilians with more obligations—led to the collapse of the social contract civilians were originally offered. Despite the promise of equality under the law, at least among the Sunni male population it regarded as full “citizens” (in its own words, “the people are as equal as the teeth of a comb”), in practice the group often gave preferential treatment to foreign fighters over local Syrian and Iraqi recruits, including higher salaries and free housing, and many local fighters deserted or defected to rival armed groups citing frustration with being treated as second-class citizens.

I describe this and other evidence of a sharp decline in the quality of the group’s personnel as an “adverse selection spiral.” Previously, the Islamic State was known for turning away prospective recruits who could not demonstrate a genuine commitment to its ideology. Its most competent and effective members were among the first to become disillusioned with its failure to live up to its own promises and to leave. To compensate for heavy casualties and desertions, it lowered its standards for new recruits and reduced the duration of their ideological and military training in order to deploy them to the frontlines as fast as possible. Over the same period in which the group was becoming more extractive and coercive, civilian opposition was growing. My evidence from interviews, local news reports, social media, and the Islamic State’s own internal records shows an increase in instances of civilian resistance—both peaceful and violent—in 2014 and 2015, including protests, complaints about the declining quality of essential services, and a graffiti campaign to deface its buildings with symbols and slogans of resistance, which eventually escalated to armed attacks against its personnel and property.

The book responds to three gaps in previous studies of the Islamic State and the broader literatures on rebel governance and state-building. First, the literature has focused heavily on how rebel legal orders are built with little to no attention to how most of these orders eventually decline and collapse; to my knowledge, no single book has comprehensively examined the rise and fall of a single insurgent legal system. Second, existing accounts rely heavily on secondary sources, and those that do include interviews focus disproportionately on third-country nationals who voluntarily left their home countries to join the group, and not on the millions of Iraqis and Syrians who were living in areas it captured and were involuntarily exposed to its rule. Third, single-country studies cannot separate the effects of insurgent governance from those of the regime it displaced. Among previous books on the Islamic State itself, the most influential accounts center the group’s ideology, propaganda, foreign recruits, or military strategy, largely through secondary sources. Missing from all of them is the perspective of the more than eight million Syrians and Iraqis who lived under its rule.

The project builds on more than three years of field research in Iraq, Syria, and Turkey between 2015 and 2021, including more than fourteen months in Iraq and sixteen months in Turkey. The draft manuscript is around 90,000 words in eleven chapters across four parts. Part I develops the argument and the origins of the Islamic State. Part II documents the legal foundations of the caliphate and the ratchet by which wartime emergency turned rules into instruments of extraction. Part III traces the group’s unraveling from within its own ranks, and under external pressure from growing civilian resistance, both peaceful and violent. Part IV tests the argument on cross-national data, examines the Taliban since 2021 as a shadow case without external war, and draws out the implications for transitional justice and reintegration.
