Insurgent Legality

Law in the Making and Breaking of the Islamic State in Iraq and Syria

Mara R. Revkin

Book manuscript in progress

Summary

The explanations most often given for the rapid rise of the self-proclaimed “Islamic State”—its recruitment of more than thirty thousand foreign fighters from at least eighty-five countries, its capture of banks, oil fields, and weapons stockpiles, and its ability to spread fear through extreme violence—do not fully account for the initial success of the state-building project it called the caliphate, or for its persistence for more than three years against the largest international military coalition since the Second World War. Nor do they explain an empirical puzzle from the earliest days of its rule. When the group captured Mosul in June 2014 after only six days of fighting, civilians were free to leave, and as many as five hundred thousand of the city’s 1.2 million residents did. Within days, tens of thousands of them began returning voluntarily, having heard from relatives who stayed that the city was calm and that services had improved. Eight months later, roughly three-quarters of Mosul’s population was still living under the group’s rule. Why did so many people stay when it was still possible to leave?

This book answers that question with a concept I call insurgent legality: a comprehensive system of law-like rules and institutions for governing people and territory, and the degree to which a governing authority’s conduct actually conforms to the rules it proclaims. Legality in this sense is not legitimacy. Most of the Syrians and Iraqis I interviewed never accepted that the Islamic State had a right to rule; they found its punishments cruel and its ideology alien. What they described nonetheless was a system—posted regulations, predictable enforcement, courts that resolved disputes and punished the group’s own members, police who could not be bribed—that made daily life calculable in a way it had not been under the Assad regime, under a series of Baghdad governments many Sunni Arabs experienced as predatory, or amid the lawless power vacuums of contested territory. Perceptions of legality, I argue, are relative rather than absolute: civilians judged the Islamic State against what they had already lived through and against the alternatives actually available to them, not against an ideal.

The evidence comes from more than three years of field research in Iraq, Syria, and Turkey: an original household survey of 1,458 residents of Mosul, in-depth interviews with more than three hundred Syrians and Iraqis who lived under the group’s rule, and an original dataset built from more than two thousand Arabic-language primary sources, including the organization’s own internal records, that maps its institutions, taxation, and conscription at the district-month level across all forty-six districts it governed. Together these sources show that the Islamic State established its legal institutions first—immediately after taking new territory, alongside subsidized bread—and that many people did perceive real improvements in public safety, sanitation, and dispute resolution during the first months of its rule.

The second half of the book concerns the unraveling. Legality is extraordinarily difficult for any rapidly growing organization to maintain, and hardest of all for one fighting a war. As the group expanded faster than it could govern, oversight failed, its most committed and competent members died or deserted, and its conduct diverged ever further from its own proclaimed rules: corruption spread, taxation became extraction, punishment became arbitrary, and the privileges granted to foreign fighters made a mockery of the promise that “the people are as equal as the teeth of a comb.” Because civilians had revised their expectations upward during the early months, this deterioration was experienced not as ordinary misgovernment but as betrayal. Records of resistance, desertion, and internal infighting were rising well before the coalition air campaign intensified. The Islamic State was already coming apart from within before it was defeated from without; military pressure accelerated a collapse whose foundations were giving way.

The argument is not monocausal. Ideology, sectarian grievance, resources, and coercion all mattered, and I assess these alternative explanations separately for the rise and for the decline. My claim is that legality is a necessary part of any adequate account of both, and the part that existing scholarship has most neglected. Its importance is also conditional: in the group’s administrative heartland, where coercive dominance was near-total and civilians had no meaningful exit, it could extract without first building legality; in contested peripheral districts, where people retained options, premature extraction bred resistance. Where control is contested, legality does work that coercion cannot—a finding with direct implications for counterinsurgency, stabilization, and transitional justice, since governments that compete with insurgents by out-coercing them are answering a question civilians are not asking.

The book joins the literatures on rebel governance and state formation by theorizing not only how insurgent institutions are built but how they decay, and it speaks to socio-legal scholarship on what law becomes without a state, and why even a violently illiberal movement finds that it cannot govern without binding itself to its own rules. Among books on the Islamic State, this one is distinguished by its vantage point: the perspectives of the more than eight million Syrians and Iraqis who lived under its rule, who are this book’s protagonists and who have been largely missing from accounts centered on ideology, propaganda, foreign recruits, and military trajectory.
